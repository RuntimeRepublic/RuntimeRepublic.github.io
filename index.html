Find out who gets paid first in your house

Most families can tell you what they spend on groceries. Very few can tell you how many governments take money from their household, or how much all of them take together. The Civic Encounters Challenge helps you find out, using an AI assistant you already have and your own records.

How it works

  1. Gather three documents: one pay stub, one utility or phone bill, and one tax notice.
  2. Count the governments named on them.
  3. Rank your real budget order: who actually gets paid first.
  4. Track your money for one full month to reach your next level.

No dollar amounts are ever shared. Your records stay yours.

Get ready now

  1. Set up a password manager first, so you never have to remember or write down a password. Free options exist.
  2. Create a free account at github.com. Use a strong, unique password from your password manager.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication: profile picture, Settings, Password and authentication.
  4. Build your master plan first: open the Master Plan Starter Kit, click Use this template, then Create a new repository. Owner: your account. Name: master-plan. Choose Private.
  5. Connect your master-plan repo to your AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, Grok or another AI that reads GitHub). Then paste this, so your AI reads the three frameworks' control documents and tells you what you are getting into:
    Read my master-plan repo and the control documents in RuntimeRepublic/AGCL-Control-Documents, RuntimeRepublic/NBBF-Control-Documents and RuntimeRepublic/NBEF-Control-Documents. In plain words, explain what I would be agreeing to in CONTROL-01-Values.md, one principle at a time. Then interview me one question at a time, with multiple-choice answers, to fill in OBJECTIVES.md and CONTROL-00-Master-Plan.md.
  6. Adopt the values only when you agree with them: put your name and date in CONTROL-01-Values.md and commit. Everyone holds the same six values, founders included.
  7. Create a private repository: the plus (+) at the top, New repository, name it Personal-Finance, choose Private, check "Add a README file", then Create.
  8. Stop there. Don't upload anything yet. On launch day we'll share a template that sets up the folders and rules.
  9. Optional: install the GitHub app on your phone and sign in.

Power Tip: pull requests are easier on your phone. In the GitHub app, tap Pull Requests, open one, read Files changed, then tap Merge.

What GitHub is, and how your information stays safe

GitHub is a free website where you keep files with a full history of every change. You see who changed what, and when, and you approve each change before it counts. Your AI assistant saves a summary there only when you tell it to.

  • Private means private. A private repository can be seen only by you and anyone you invite.
  • Originals stay with you. Bills, pay stubs and statements stay on your own computer or phone. Only summaries go to GitHub.
  • Never stored: passwords, Social Security numbers, or full account numbers. Accounts are named by their last four digits only.
  • You stay in control. Your AI never moves money, pays bills or changes your accounts, and nothing is shared unless you say so.
  • Lock the door. A strong password plus two-factor authentication protects your account.

What is shared, and with whom

You decide what leaves your hands. There are four levels.

  • Only you: your original bills, pay stubs and statements stay on your own computer or phone. Your private GitHub repository holds your budget, your dashboard and your AI's summaries.
  • Your Study Circle, only when you push it: broad category figures for the study, such as housing, food, and taxes and fees. Your AI prepares a file, you read it first, and you send it yourself. It never includes your name, accounts, employer or individual bills. Scheduled tasks can track your budget, but they never send study data.
  • The public: group counts on the Study Circle dashboard and the weekly wins, like "7 members closed their week." Never one person's numbers. Your name appears only if you say yes.
  • No one, ever: passwords, Social Security numbers and full account numbers are never stored or shared.
