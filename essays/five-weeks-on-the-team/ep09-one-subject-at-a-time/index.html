One Subject at a Time

Five Weeks on the Team · Episode 9 of 10 · about 7 minutes · Draft under review

The Monday after Jake moved City Council to the top of his list, Maria asked the question nobody else had thought to ask. "What does an exploratory committee actually have to do? Are there rules for just thinking about it?"

The app didn't answer from memory. It opened the Kansas statutes and the Governmental Ethics Commission's regulations and read them with the team, line by line, the way they read a bank statement.

What they found surprised them. Kansas law doesn't have a special "exploratory" status at all. A person becomes a candidate on the day he meets one of the tests in the law, such as taking in money or spending money to run. Within ten days after that, he must appoint a treasurer. Until he does, he can't take a single contribution or spend a single dollar on the campaign. The app also read the line that settled what Dale had offered weeks earlier: a candidate may serve as his own treasurer.

"So I could do it myself," Jake said. "That keeps it simple."

The app logged it as an option and marked it verified, with the source. Exploring with no money changing hands was still free. The moment money moved, the clock started.

Then, at the Sunday meeting, Pastor Dan raised his hand. "Before you decide to be your own treasurer," he said, "you should know something. Three people in this church have already told me they would be willing to serve as treasurer for somebody next year. They've been hoping to find a way to serve that isn't the nursery or the parking lot."

He named them, with their permission. Helen had been a bank teller for thirty years. Marcus was a young accountant who had joined one of the church teams in the spring. Earl was a deacon who had kept the church's books through two building projects.

Jake was quiet for a moment. "Three people want to keep the books for a campaign that doesn't exist yet?"

"Three people want to serve," Dan said. "Your campaign just happens to be the first one in the building."

The app helped the team see how it could work. Kansas law allows one treasurer. If a candidate appoints a candidate committee, he also appoints a chairperson. So two of the three could hold the legal roles. The third role came from the team's own controls, not the law: a reviewer who reconciles the treasurer's books every week, the same way every member's books get a second set of eyes at close-out. No one handles money and checks it too. Helen would be treasurer, because the job is counting cash. Earl would be chairperson. Marcus would be the reviewer, and he asked to start before there was anything to review, so the process would be ready on the first day.

Dale, who had offered first, was the happiest person in the room. "I'll stick to my own Saturday books," he said. "Those I understand."

Jake still didn't appoint anyone. He wrote it down as a plan, dated, with three names and three yeses, and marked it "not yet." No money, no candidate. But the plan was ready.

Then the conversation turned, because of a reply card.

It came from their congressman's office, in answer to the team's federal letter about withholding. Most of it was a form letter. But one sentence stopped Gary: the congressman had voted for the year-end funding package "to keep the government open." Gary looked it up. The package was an omnibus bill, many spending bills rolled into one.

"Why is that even a thing?" Gary asked. "Why would anyone put everything into one bill?"

The team decided to spend a whole meeting on that question. Like every big question, it started with the facts.

The app pulled one example from the public record, the omnibus that funded the federal government for 2023. It was 4,155 pages and spent $1.7 trillion. The text came out around 1:30 in the morning on a Tuesday, days before the Friday deadline to fund the government. Then the app showed them something closer to home. The Kansas Constitution says no bill may contain more than one subject, except appropriation bills and bills that revise or codify the statutes, and the subject has to be in the title. Even appropriation bills have a limit in Kansas: the courts have said they can't carry subjects wholly unrelated to their main purpose. The federal Constitution has no such rule. There is a bill in Congress right now, the One Subject at a Time Act, that would require each bill to have one subject. It was introduced and sent to committee, and that's where it sits.

Then the team did what the team does. They tested the omnibus against the control documents, one control at a time. The app read each rule out loud, and the team voted on each one: met, partly met or not met.

The first was the Scripture chief control. Its second principle says that what is authorized is permitted, and what isn't authorized is forbidden. Ruth asked how anyone could tell what was authorized in four thousand pages read in three days. Not met. Its third principle says no one may add requirements beyond the written standard. Earl, sitting in as a guest, said a bill nobody can finish reading is the easiest place to add things. Not met.

Next came Authority and Expiration. Every authority must declare its purpose, scope, duration, success measures, exit condition and constitutional basis. The omnibus declared a bill number and a fiscal year, which counts as a duration. The rest was buried. Partly met. Another rule in the same control says funding does not create authority. The team couldn't tell how many programs in the package were being funded after their authorization had run out. The app marked that as an open item to research, not a guess.

Signal and Non-Interference says delay is interference and that results can't be buried in summary documents. A 1:30 a.m. release before a Friday vote looked like both. Not met.

Debt and Intergenerational Liability says debt must be contained to its own node, and failure in one node can't be offset by another. One bill for everything is the opposite of containment. Not met.

Constitutional Compatibility requires every node to cite its constitutional basis, and requires unconstitutional parts to be severable. Jake raised his hand on that one. He had learned from his own court research that the President has no line-item veto. The Supreme Court struck down the one Congress tried. So with a bundle, the choice is all of it or none of it. Not met.

Exit and Disengagement made Maria laugh. "A congressman can't exit one part of the bill. He has to take the whole thing or shut down the government." Not met.

Amendment and Version Control requires every change to declare its scope and to sit through a notice period before it takes effect. The team's own rule for its controls is seven days. The omnibus had days, not a week. Not met.

Then they reached Transparency and Public Ledger. The text was published, and anyone could download it. That was the one bright spot. Partly met.

When they finished, the omnibus had partly met two controls and failed almost everything else. The app showed the scorecard and then, as it always does, the other side. The red team said the controls are stricter than the Constitution itself, so failing them proves only that the controls are strict. The blue team said an omnibus is often the only thing that passes before a shutdown deadline, and a shutdown has costs too. Every family on the team had felt one. The team wrote both down. Then Gary said what everyone was thinking. "If the only way to avoid a shutdown is to vote on four thousand pages nobody read, the deadline is the problem, not the solution."

They wrote the letter that night. It was short. It thanked the congressman's office for answering. It said, in plain words, what the team had found and which controls the omnibus failed. Then it asked two questions. Will you cosponsor the One Subject at a Time Act? And will you commit to voting no on any spending bill that hasn't been public for at least seven days, and tell us in writing if you won't?

They tucked a reply card inside, with three boxes. I will cosponsor. I will meet with your team. I disagree, and here is why.

Jake signed it as a citizen, not a candidate. Then he opened his Exploratory tab, went to the Kansas House row, and added one line to his own side: Kansas already has a one-subject rule. Find out how often appropriation bills test it.

Helen, Earl and Marcus each got a note from Jake that week. It said: "Not yet. But thank you. When it's time, you'll be the first to know."
