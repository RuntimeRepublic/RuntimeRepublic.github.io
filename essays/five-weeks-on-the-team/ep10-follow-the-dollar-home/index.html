Follow the Dollar Home

Five Weeks on the Team · Episode 10 of 10 · about 7 minutes · Draft under review

On the first of December, the envelopes came.

Every member of every team who owned property got one: the county's property tax statement, with a breakdown of the state, county, city, school district and other levies stacked on top of one another. Most years, people open it, sigh and put it on the fridge. This year was different, because this year they had a team, and they had spent eleven months learning to follow a dollar.

Helen went first. She is the retired bank teller who had offered to keep Jake's books someday, a widow who owns her small house outright. She held her statement up to the camera at the Sunday meeting and read every line out loud. Then she asked the app to do what it does with every receipt: trace each levy to the government that set it, and follow it as far as the public record allows.

The app drew it out. A line for the state. A line for the county. A line for the city. A line for the school district, which was the biggest by far. A few smaller lines for districts most of them had never heard of. Each line had a name, a rate, a body that voted for it, and the people who sit on that body. The app put every one of those officeholders on the contacts strip, right next to the ones who had answered the team's letters during the year.

Then Tom and Karen found something that surprised them. They have a mortgage with an escrow account, so their statement didn't come to them at all. It went to the mortgage company, which paid it out of the escrow money folded into their monthly payment. "We've owned this house for twenty years," Tom said, "and we've never actually seen our property tax bill." The app added it to their card under the same note it uses for the employer half of Social Security: paid on your behalf and never shown to you.

That was when the follow-the-dollar trail exploded across the church teams. By Wednesday, every team in the city had traced its own statements. By Friday, the team in another state had done the same with theirs. The missionary family in the Philippines sent a photo of their own local tax notice, traced the same way.

Then Helen asked the question that changed the tone of everything. "What happens if I can't pay it?"

The app didn't soften the answer, and it didn't guess. It read the Kansas statutes with her. The first half is due by December 20, and after that it starts drawing interest. If the taxes stay unpaid, the property can be sold to the county at a tax sale. The owner gets a period to redeem it by paying everything owed plus interest, and if it isn't redeemed by the end of that period, the county can foreclose and sell it. The app marked how long the redemption period would be for a home like Helen's as an open item, to check against the statute's exact terms, rather than say it from memory.

Helen was quiet for a long time. Then she said what a lot of people were thinking. "So I paid off this house twelve years ago. And I still can lose it if I can't pay the county. Then what did I buy?"

Within a week, people across the teams were saying it in their own words, and they posted it on the dashboards. If you can lose it for not paying, the tax is a liability that sits on top of your ownership. The team's control documents already had a rule about it, Rule C8: no recurring tax may be imposed on owned property without a matching ownership claim by the taxing authority. That week, for the first time, people didn't need anyone to explain the rule. They had the envelope in their hands.

The letters went out faster than any letters that year: to the county commissioners, the city council, the school board and their state legislators. Each one traced the levy, named the body that voted for it, and asked the question Helen asked. Each one had a reply card inside.

Then Jake found the last piece. He was working on the Kansas House row of his Exploratory tab when he noticed a property tax bill from the last session. Its history didn't make sense. The bill number had started the session as a bill about something else entirely, had a hearing on that subject, and passed one chamber. Then, late in the session, its contents were stripped out and replaced with entirely new language on a different subject. The new version never had a hearing of its own.

The app had a name for it, because Topeka does. They call it a "gut and go." Lawmakers strip a bill of its contents and replace them with new language, often on a different subject. It isn't rare. In 2017, nearly a quarter of the bills enacted in Kansas started out on a different subject. The Kansas Legislature has no rule against it, although Missouri and other states do. Bills to curb it in Kansas have never succeeded.

"We have a one-subject rule in our constitution," Jake said. "And we have a way to swap the subject after the public has spoken."

The team ran it through the same controls they had used on the omnibus. Signal and Non-Interference says delay is interference and narratives may not substitute for the record. A hearing on one subject that stands in for a vote on another failed that test. Amendment and Version Control says every change must declare its scope and give notice before it takes effect. A gut and go does neither. Transparency says every node must be visible to everyone, and a bill whose number doesn't match its history isn't visible to a citizen trying to follow it. Failed, failed, failed.

Jake added one line to his own side of the Kansas House row: no gut and go, and if I ever vote for one, the team should vote me out.

That was the last Sunday of the five weeks. Pastor Dan closed the meeting, but he didn't preach. He just asked everyone in the room to say one thing they had learned this year. Dale said a dash camera and a hash can tell the truth when you can't. Jake said the law is open to anyone willing to read it. Tom and Karen said a family can work its goals the same way it works its budget. Gary said government gets paid first, and that he wants the family paid first. Helen said she had paid off her house, and she wanted it to stay hers.

Then they turned off the camera, and that's where these stories end.

But before you go, I want to talk to you directly.

Everything in these ten episodes is a story. The people aren't real. The process is. Somewhere in your house there's a pay stub you've never really read. There's a receipt with a tax line you've never followed, a bill with fees you've never asked about, and maybe an envelope from the county you put on the fridge with a sigh. Every one of those is a dollar you earned, and you have every right to know where it went.

You don't have to do it alone. That's the whole point. A team is three to six people. It can be your family, your friends or a few people from church. You meet once a week. You each track your own money, and you share only what you choose to share. You trace one tax at a time. You write one letter at a time, with a reply card inside, to the person who set that tax. You don't need a degree, and you don't need permission. You need a team.

Some of the people who answer will disagree with you. Some won't answer at all. A few will check the box that says "I will meet with your team." Every answer goes on the record, and the record belongs to you.

So here's the ask. Before next Sunday, call two people you trust. Tell them you want to know where your money goes, and ask them to find out with you. That's a team. Start one.
